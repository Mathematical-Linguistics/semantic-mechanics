## 1. A Question About What We Keep

Greed is often presented as a private defect: one person wants too much. But what counts as “too much,” who gets to define enough, and what happens to everyone else when one person or institution accumulates control? This lecture follows a change in scale:

1. A story of assault and the struggle to know what happened.
2. Listening as attention, pressure, and a contest over who controls the subject.
3. Grief as a relation to loss rather than a failure to recover.
4. Resource defense in animals, described without projecting human moral categories onto them.
5. Epidemiology and behavioral economics as tools for mapping and preventing harm.
6. Class, racial hierarchy, debt, reparations, reconstruction, and basic income as questions of institutional design.

The through-line is **resource security**: bodies, attention, credibility, money, land, time, and the ability to refuse. Greed is one possible name for harmful accumulation, but the analysis must ask what is accumulated, by whom, through what rules, and at whose expense.

### Learning objectives

By the end of the lecture, students should be able to:

- Explain how “toxic masculinity” names harmful norms and reward systems, not an essence shared by men.
- Analyze listening as a practice with power, boundaries, and accountability.
- Distinguish ordinary grief from a clinical disorder and from social abandonment.
- Analyze resource defense and cooperation as behaviors humans share with other animals, while accounting for the distinctive reach of human institutions and culture.
- Distinguish fixed-sum competition from positive-sum cooperation, and ask who benefits when the total surplus grows.
- Explain why a convincing cloned voice is not proof of identity, consent, or viewpoint, and apply practical verification steps.
- Use an epidemiologic frame to study distributions and causes of harm without calling greed, racism, or grief a “disease of the mind.”
- Apply behavioral-economics tools to prevention while identifying when “nudging” becomes paternalism or surveillance.
- Evaluate debt relief, reparations, reconstruction, and universal basic income as distinct approaches to repair and security.

## 2. “Toxic”: From Poison to a Social Diagnosis

The word *toxic* evokes a substance that harms through exposure. In contemporary social use, it often describes a relationship, norm, institution, or environment that causes damage. The metaphor is vivid but can obscure causation: substances have doses and pathways; social harms have histories, incentives, power relations, and people who can choose differently.

“Toxic masculinity” has not had one stable meaning. The phrase circulated in late-twentieth-century men's-movement discourse, including mythopoetic writing, and later became common in feminist, psychological, and popular debate. In present research and practice, it is most useful when it describes **restrictive norms**—for example, entitlement, dominance, emotional suppression, sexual coercion, or the treatment of vulnerability as weakness—and the social rewards that can sustain them. It is not a synonym for masculinity, men, or male identity. Masculinities vary across history and culture; people can resist, revise, or reproduce norms.

The distinction matters in the 2022 *This American Life* episode, Act One, “Known Unknowns,” which tells the story of Vienna Luke seeking an account of an assault by her brother-in-law. The episode describes her memory gaps, the family’s prolonged avoidance, and her eventual, police-supported recorded meeting in which Justin acknowledges drugging her, removing her clothes, photographing her, and other acts. The story is not offered as a template for survivors: the meeting was an unusually supported investigative operation, and its result does not imply that a survivor must confront an assailant, obtain a confession, report to police, or pursue closure.

Justin’s descriptions of “lines” he believed he had not crossed and his detours into the general problem of pornography illustrate a crucial distinction between **self-image** and **accountability**. A person can preserve a story of being good by defining harm narrowly, treating intent as exculpatory, or replacing a specific act with a broad cultural explanation. Explanation can illuminate conditions; it does not erase agency or responsibility.

The phrase “toxic masculinity” can help name a pattern, but it can also become a shortcut that stigmatizes men as a group, overlooks differences in power, or mistakes an explanation for a solution. A careful analysis identifies:

| Question | What to examine |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Norm** | What behavior is framed as masculine, respectable, or entitled? |
| **Reward** | Who gains approval, status, access, money, or impunity for following it? |
| **Cost** | Who bears bodily, emotional, social, or economic harm? |
| **Institution** | What procedure or power imbalance protects the behavior? |
| **Alternative** | What norm, relationship, or accountability practice could change the outcome? |

This keeps the object of critique precise: a harmful practice and its enabling conditions, not an entire identity category.

## 3. Aggressive Listening: Attention, Territory, and Consent

The Bureau for Listening’s project frames “aggressive listening” as a kind of **auditory staring**: sustained attention that can affect, challenge, or contest the territory of what may be said. It deliberately resists a closed definition. The useful tension is that listening is often described as passive or benevolent, while attention can also be intrusive, demanding, and politically consequential.

In the episode, Vienna repeatedly returns the conversation to the question of what happened when Justin shifts toward abstractions. Her insistence is not a general listening technique to prescribe to survivors; it is an action made possible by preparation, a clear goal, and a police team monitoring the operation. Her listening is active because she controls the question and refuses the other speaker’s attempts to change the frame.

We can describe the exchange as a **frame-control problem**:

$$
\text{Accountability} =
\text{specific act}
+ \text{specific agent}
+ \text{specific consequence}
+ \text{response to the harmed person}
$$

When any term is replaced by a vague generality—“culture,” “addiction,” “mistakes,” “everyone does it”—the account may lose the actor and the consequence. Listening closely means hearing both what is said and what a speaker’s framing leaves out.

But aggressive listening has an ethical edge. Attention can be care; it can also be extraction. A listener should ask:

- Who set the terms of this conversation?
- Is the speaker safe to stop, pause, or decline?
- Is the listener seeking understanding, evidence, confession, or spectacle?
- Who controls the recording, retelling, and circulation of the account?
- What responsibilities follow from hearing?

The episode’s hidden recording is part of a specific police-supported investigation. It should not be generalized into advice to secretly record people. Recording law, safety risks, consent, and an individual’s goals vary. A listener’s obligation is not to force disclosure; it is to respect agency, avoid replacing the speaker’s account with speculation, and respond to concrete safety needs.

### Voice doppelgängers: listening is not authentication

A **voice doppelgänger** is a generated or edited voice that imitates a person’s vocal identity. It may make words sound as if they came from someone who never said them, or make a genuine recording appear to express a perspective the speaker did not hold. This matters for aggressive listening: sustained attention to an audio artifact is not the same as listening to the person whose voice it imitates. A voice can prompt intimacy, fear, or trust, but timbral resemblance alone cannot establish identity, authorship, consent, context, or intent.

Authorized voice synthesis can also support augmentative communication, preserve a person’s chosen voice when speech is changing, enable translation, and serve creative performance. Those benefits make meaningful consent and continuing control important, not optional.

The ethical questions concern more than whether a model can imitate a voice:

| Issue | Questions for creators, platforms, and listeners |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Permission** | Did the person knowingly agree to the use of their voice, for this purpose and audience? |
| **Scope** | Does permission cover only a specific, reviewed recording, or open-ended generation of new statements? |
| **Disclosure** | Can listeners tell that speech is synthetic or materially edited? |
| **Control** | Can the person review, limit, or revoke future use? What happens to stored recordings and models? |
| **Power and harm** | Who is paid, impersonated, made vulnerable, or burdened with disproving the output? |
| **After death** | Who may authorize a replica, and how are the person’s wishes and the interests of family and audience represented? |

Consent to contribute a voice sample is not consent to every sentence that can be generated from it. Nor does an authentic recording guarantee an authentic perspective: it may be edited, taken out of context, or spoken under pressure. For high-stakes listening, ask who made the recording, how it reached you, whether it is labeled, and what independent evidence supports the claim. Treat a purported voice message as an artifact to verify—not as direct testimony—until its source and context are established.

**Practical protection when a voice message requests money, access, or secrecy:**

1. Pause. Urgency, secrecy, and instructions not to check with anyone are warning signs; do not transfer money or share passwords, identity documents, or one-time security codes based on a voice alone.
2. Verify through a channel you already trust: call a number saved independently or contact the person through a separate, established account. Do not rely on the incoming number, caller ID, or a link supplied in the message.
3. If appropriate, use a family check-in phrase agreed in advance, but treat it as one signal—not an infallible test. Do not use a memorable phrase as an account password or authentication code.
4. For financial or account changes, use the institution’s official app or contact information and ask for time to verify. If money or credentials were already sent, contact the bank or service promptly through its official channel.
5. Preserve the original message, sender details, time, and surrounding context; report suspected impersonation to the relevant platform and, where appropriate, local authorities. Avoid forwarding the clip as if it were authentic.

Voice samples already online cannot be made un-cloneable, and there is no reliable “listen harder” test that proves a voice is genuine. Automated detectors and audible artifacts can help triage but may miss sophisticated edits or flag real speech. Provenance labels and watermarks can add context when present, but their absence does not prove authenticity and their presence does not prove that the content is truthful. The core defense is layered verification and a norm that no one should be pressured to treat a voiceprint as consent or proof.

## 4. Insecurity, Active Inference, and the Limits of the Model

The companion notebook models insecurity as a strong negative prior that dominates ambiguous evidence. It sketches how avoidance can preserve a belief by reducing opportunities to gather new information, and how supported behavioral experiments may provide disconfirming evidence. This is a useful computational analogy for some patterns of anxious prediction:

$$
\text{posterior belief} \propto
\text{prior belief} \times \text{likelihood of observed evidence}
$$

An agent who expects rejection may interpret ambiguous social cues as confirmation, then avoid the situations that could test that expectation. The notebook’s language of precision weighting and active sampling offers a way to ask how beliefs are maintained and updated.

However, a model is not a diagnosis, and several distinctions are essential:

1. **A survivor’s uncertainty is not necessarily an inaccurate belief.** Memory gaps after trauma are not proof that an assault did or did not happen. The responsibility for an assault lies with the person who committed it, not with the survivor’s inference process.
2. **Evidence is not always safe to seek.** In an abuse or coercive relationship, approaching the source of uncertainty can increase danger. “Exposure” is not a universal prescription.
3. **A social environment can withhold information or credibility.** Not every locked belief is maintained by an individual’s avoidance; institutions and other people can actively prevent access to evidence or recognition.
4. **The notebook’s equations are explanatory simplifications.** Its Kalman-gain and free-energy diagrams do not by themselves validate a clinical treatment, establish a universal brain mechanism, or capture the social history of trauma.

The best transfer is modest: uncertainty can be worsened by avoidance, but not all uncertainty is self-generated; gathering information can support learning, but only when it is safe, wanted, and proportionate. In Vienna’s case, preparation, law enforcement, evidence, and her own deliberate questions mattered. The episode also shows that facts did not automatically restore the relationship she hoped to repair.

## 5. Grief: Loss Without a Deadline

Grief is a response to loss, not an error that must be corrected. It may follow death, but also the loss of trust, bodily safety, a relationship, a future one expected, or a family’s former shape. The episode holds several kinds of grief at once: Vienna’s grief over what was done to her, the family’s altered rituals, and the hoped-for return of closeness with Sunny.

Grief does not proceed in a universal sequence. Stage models can be evocative descriptions, but they are not a timetable that every grieving person must complete. People may move among anger, numbness, yearning, relief, and ordinary daily life; they may continue a bond with the person or life they lost. A continuing bond is not necessarily denial, and a public or legal outcome does not guarantee emotional repair.

Clinical diagnosis has a narrower role. Prolonged grief disorder describes a persistent, impairing grief response that meets specific criteria in a particular diagnostic system. It should not be used to pathologize ordinary sadness, cultural mourning, or an inconvenient demand for accountability. Nor should a lack of visible grief be treated as evidence that no harm occurred.

For discussion, separate three questions:

- **What was lost?** Name the relationship, safety, time, income, community, or future.
- **What support is available?** Consider chosen family, material resources, ritual, therapy, legal advocacy, and time.
- **What remains unresolved because another person or institution will not act?** Grief may coexist with a claim for justice; it is not a substitute for it.

“Closure” is often imagined as an internal finish line. A more humane goal may be increased safety, room to remember or not remember, the ability to set boundaries, and a life not organized entirely around the harm.

## 6. Resource Defense, Cooperation, and Zero-Sum Thinking Across Animal Life

Humans are animals. We share with other species basic needs and evolved capacities for competition, cooperation, attachment, defense, and exchange. Human beings also defend food, shelter, territory, offspring, and valued social positions. Those behaviors are shaped—not simply dictated—by ecology, development, culture, and institutions. Other animals likewise cooperate, share, form alliances, and negotiate access. Neither competition nor cooperation belongs exclusively to humans.

In behavioral ecology, researchers might ask what resource is at stake, how contest costs compare to likely benefits, whether a resource is defendable, and what signals or escalation strategies animals use. A small lizard defending a basking site and a human landlord enforcing a lease may both involve access and exclusion, but the settings, capacities, and meanings differ. “Greed” is a moral and cultural interpretation, not a neutral zoological diagnosis.

The comparison is useful when it helps us ask about resource access, conflict costs, and cooperation without making a natural fact out of a political arrangement:

| Animal-behavior question | Human institutional analogue | Important difference |
| :--- | :--- | :--- |
| Is a resource scarce, clumped, or renewable? | How are housing, food, land, or income distributed? | Scarcity may be socially produced or politically maintained. |
| What is the energetic or injury cost of defense? | What bodily, emotional, or civic costs accompany competition? | Human rules can redistribute costs and authorize coercion. |
| Who can access the resource or signal ownership? | Who has legal title, bargaining power, or recognized claims? | Human ownership is constituted by institutions and history. |
| What forms of cooperation or sharing are stable? | Can people create reciprocal arrangements, public goods, or shared infrastructure? | Human cooperation can be organized across large populations and generations. |

### Fixed-sum contests and “why can’t we grow the pie?”

A **zero-sum game**, in the strict sense, has a fixed total payoff: one participant’s gain is another’s loss. Some contests are genuinely fixed-sum over a particular scarce resource—for example, one indivisible object can be awarded to only one claimant. Many social and economic situations are not fixed-sum. Cooperation, technology, public health, and shared infrastructure can create **positive-sum gains**, where total benefits grow.

But “grow the pie” is not a complete answer to distribution. Total output can expand while ownership and bargaining power remain concentrated; the gains may flow upward while pollution, unpaid care, animal suffering, or precarious labor are treated as external costs. Conversely, redistributing a fixed resource does not necessarily create new resources, but may be needed for fairness and security. Ask both:

- **Can the total surplus grow?** What cooperation, innovation, or public investment could make more value or reduce avoidable loss?
- **How is the surplus distributed?** Who owns the assets, captures the gains, bears the risks, and has the power to refuse?
- **What is being counted as a cost?** Does the “larger pie” depend on unpriced environmental damage, coerced labor, or harm to other animals?

Human institutions extend resource defense beyond immediate physical contests. Property law, inheritance, credit, labor markets, and political voice can store claims across time and make exclusion durable. Cumulative culture can also support reciprocal exchange, collective care, and rules that limit domination. These capacities do not make humans categorically separate from nature; they make our social arrangements unusually consequential and open to redesign.

An animal analogy should therefore illuminate, not excuse. “Competition made me do it” naturalizes a choice; “people are just territorial” naturalizes an institution. The better questions are what conditions elicit defensive behavior, when cooperation is possible, and who can change the rules.

## 7. Epidemiology Without Calling Society Sick

Epidemiology studies the distribution and determinants of health-related states in populations. Its methods can help investigate the **patterning of harm**: where exposure occurs, who is more likely to experience it, which institutions amplify risk, and what interventions reduce incidence or severity. They do not establish that greed, racism, masculinity, or grief are mental diseases.

A population-health frame might map:

$$
\text{conditions and exposures}
\rightarrow \text{patterns of harm}
\rightarrow \text{unequal outcomes}
\rightarrow \text{prevention and repair}
$$

For example, researchers could study how housing insecurity, workplace power, social isolation, access to care, or institutional impunity correlate with violence and distress. These are associations to investigate, not proof that any single exposure causes an individual act. Avoid confusing a risk factor with a diagnosis or blaming people who experience the outcome.

The World Health Organization describes social determinants as the conditions in which people are born, grow, live, work, and age, including access to power, money, and resources. That framing shifts inquiry from “What is wrong with these people?” to “Which conditions distribute risk and protection, and how can they change?” The metaphor of a “social pathogen” can sometimes draw attention to contagious norms or repeated institutional practices, but it can also medicalize dissent, stigmatize groups, and hide agents who benefit. Prefer direct language—violence, coercion, exclusion, exploitation, or unequal exposure—when it names the mechanism more accurately.

An ethical population analysis should state its outcome, population, timeframe, comparison group, and limits; distinguish correlation from cause; avoid treating group averages as individual destinies; and involve affected communities in interpreting results. The aim is prevention and equitable protection, not surveillance or labeling a community as diseased.

## 8. Behavioral Economics: Steering Away from Harm

Behavioral economics asks how decisions are shaped by attention, defaults, framing, habits, limited time, and uncertainty—not just stable preferences and perfect information. Prospect theory, for example, describes how choices under risk can depend on reference points and losses. Scarcity can narrow attention toward urgent demands, while unequal bargaining power can make a nominal “choice” costly to refuse.

These insights can inform harm prevention:

- **Change defaults:** Make consent, privacy, and safe reporting the default rather than an extra burden.
- **Reduce friction:** Offer accessible routes to housing, health care, legal advice, and income support.
- **Make consequences legible:** State who is accountable, what will happen next, and where a complaint goes.
- **Design for repair:** Provide predictable, timely restitution and accessible non-carceral supports.
- **Protect meaningful choice:** Preserve opt-out, confidentiality, due process, and avenues to contest institutional decisions.

Yet a “nudge” is not automatically benevolent. A default can help people claim a benefit, or quietly enroll them in surveillance. A reminder can improve safety, or shift responsibility from a powerful institution to an individual with less power. Behavioral tools should be judged by who designed them, who benefits, who bears error, whether people can refuse, and whether the intervention changes the harmful environment or merely teaches vulnerable people to navigate it.

### The cost of harm

Accounting for harm requires more than a price tag. A cost map can include:

| Harm domain | Examples to consider |
| :--- | :--- |
| **Physical** | Injury, pain, disability, medical care, lost mobility |
| **Cognitive and emotional** | Fear, sleep disruption, impaired concentration, grief, loss of trust |
| **Social** | Isolation, family rupture, lost education, reduced civic participation |
| **Economic** | Unpaid care, missed work, lower wages, legal costs, displacement |
| **Ecological and interspecies** | Habitat loss, animal injury, disrupted food systems |
| **Institutional** | Investigation, court processes, reduced trust, resources diverted from prevention |

These domains interact. A person may face a physical injury, cognitive load, lost wages, and isolation at once. Monetizing some costs can help compare policy choices, but it cannot fully represent dignity, consent, or irreplaceable loss. A responsible analysis shows what its accounting leaves out and never makes a harmed person responsible for proving that their pain is expensive enough to matter.

## 9. Class, Race, and the Unequal Distribution of Credibility

All humans belong to one species, *Homo sapiens*. “Race” is not a set of discrete biological subspecies, but racial categories have been historically made and politically enforced; they have real consequences because institutions distribute safety, wealth, credibility, and opportunity through them. Saying “we are all one human race” can affirm shared humanity, but it cannot by itself explain or remedy racialized inequality.

Class describes relations to resources, work, ownership, and bargaining power; it is not interchangeable with race. The categories intersect and vary across place and history. A class analysis that ignores racialized dispossession can erase how wealth was accumulated. A racial analysis that ignores labor and property can miss how class power reproduces inequality. Neither should assume that every person in a group has the same experience.

The episode’s family conflict makes credibility visible at an intimate scale: who is believed, whose comfort is prioritized, and who is expected to keep the family together. At a societal scale, similar questions appear in access to legal remedy, recognition of workplace injury, treatment by institutions, and the historical valuation of whose labor and life count.

The lecture’s key question is not whether people are “naturally” competitive. It is how rules turn some people’s claims into enforceable rights while leaving others with the cost, the debt, or the burden of proof.

## 10. Debt, Wage Slavery, and Repair

“Wage slavery” is a polemical phrase used to criticize workers’ dependence on wages under conditions where exit is difficult. It can illuminate coercion, low bargaining power, and the concentration of ownership. It must not be used as if wage labor and chattel slavery were identical: chattel slavery legally treated people as property and was enforced through racialized violence, family separation, and the denial of personhood. Debt bondage and peonage are also distinct systems, though they can intersect with wage dependence and coercive labor.

Debt is both a contract and a relation of power. Who can borrow cheaply, whose debt is forgiven, what collateral can be seized, and which obligations survive crisis are political questions. When essential needs require high-cost borrowing, or when a worker cannot leave without losing housing, health care, or immigration security, “choice” needs to be examined in context.

### Poetic justice, accountability, and reparations

“Poetic justice” often means an outcome that symbolically fits an injustice: a wrongdoer receives a fitting consequence. Symbolic recognition can matter, but symmetry or revenge is not the same as repair. A reparative framework asks:

1. What happened, and who had the power to cause or prevent it?
2. What losses and continuing effects can be identified?
3. What forms of acknowledgment, restitution, compensation, or institutional change are due?
4. Who participates in setting the remedy and evaluating whether it works?

Reparations are not simply charity. They address wrongful taking, exclusion, or state-backed harm through material and institutional measures. Their design may include direct payments, return of land or property, debt relief, public investment, access to services, truth-telling, memorialization, and guarantees against recurrence. These mechanisms answer different harms; a single payment or apology may be inadequate.

### Reconstruction: the United States and beyond

U.S. Reconstruction after the Civil War (roughly 1865–1877) brought constitutional amendments abolishing slavery, establishing birthright citizenship, and prohibiting denial of voting rights on the basis of race, color, or previous condition of servitude; women remained excluded from voting. It also saw Black political participation and institution-building. Its possibilities were sharply constrained and then attacked through white supremacist violence, political retreat, and the withdrawal of federal protection. Reconstruction should not be reduced to a “failed experiment”: its gains, limits, and reversal reveal how rights require durable institutions and enforcement.

“Reconstruction” also names different projects elsewhere. Postwar rebuilding in Europe and Japan involved state capacity, investment, and security arrangements; South Africa’s post-apartheid transition paired political change with truth-seeking and a Reconstruction and Development Programme, while debate over land, wealth, and material redress continued. These histories are not interchangeable. In each, ask whose security was restored, who controlled resources, which harms were acknowledged, and whether the project transformed the distribution of power or mainly stabilized it.

### Universal basic income: promise and peril

A universal basic income (UBI) generally means regular cash paid individually, without a means test or work requirement. It promises income security, administrative simplicity, greater bargaining power, and time for care, study, or creative work. It may reduce the stigma and cliffs that can accompany targeted benefits.

But “UBI” covers many different proposals. A modest payment cannot replace health care, disability support, housing, child care, or public services. If it substitutes for those programs, it can leave people worse off. A poorly funded transfer could also be offset by higher rents, prices, or cuts elsewhere. A universal payment’s fiscal cost, tax design, inflationary conditions, and distributional effects matter. Evidence from cash-transfer trials can inform these debates, but short pilots are not equivalent to a permanent national UBI.

Evaluate any proposal by asking: How much, how often, and to whom? What happens to existing benefits and public services? How is it financed? Does it improve workers’ ability to refuse dangerous or degrading work? Does it reach people without stable addresses or bank access? Does it complement reparations and targeted support, or get offered as a substitute for them?

## 11. Synthesis: From Hoarding to Shared Security

The word “greed” can name a judgment, but it cannot do the whole analysis. An adequate account connects motive, opportunity, institutional permission, distribution of harm, and possible alternatives:

$$
\text{harmful accumulation}
= \text{concentrated control}
+ \text{externalized costs}
+ \text{weak accountability}
$$

This is a heuristic, not a diagnostic formula. Its purpose is to direct attention toward the social arrangements that make harm profitable, invisible, or difficult to contest. The repair question is not simply “How can we make individuals less greedy?” It is “How can we build conditions in which safety, resources, credibility, and the power to refuse are not hoarded?”

## 13. Laboratory Exercise: A Harm-and-Repair Map

Choose a case study from a public report, a historical source, a synthetic-media incident, or a fictional scenario. Do not use a classmate’s personal experience.

1. **Define the outcome:** Specify the harm without using a vague label such as “toxicity.”
2. **Map exposure and protection:** Identify who is more exposed, who has protective resources, and what evidence supports the map.
3. **Trace the mechanism:** Distinguish individual action, social norm, institutional rule, and historical condition.
4. **Estimate costs:** List physical, cognitive/emotional, social, economic, and ecological effects. Mark which cannot responsibly be monetized.
5. **Design a prevention step:** Use a behavioral or institutional change. State who controls it, who can opt out, and how success will be measured.
6. **Design a repair step:** Specify acknowledgment, restitution, compensation, service, or institutional reform. Explain who helps decide and how the remedy avoids replacing public obligations with charity.
7. **Stress-test the proposal:** Ask who might be excluded, surveilled, burdened, or harmed by the intervention.
8. **For a voice-cloning case:** Separate the audio’s apparent identity from verified source, consent, context, and intended audience. Propose a verification path that does not require trusting the disputed recording.

**Deliverable:** A one-page causal map and a short explanation of one uncertainty that remains. The goal is not to produce a perfect measure of suffering; it is to make the reasoning, evidence, and limits visible.
